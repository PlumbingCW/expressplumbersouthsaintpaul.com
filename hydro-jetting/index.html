What hydro jetting actually does

A jetter feeds a hose with a rotating nozzle into the line. Water leaves the nozzle at high pressure in a pattern that cuts grease, scrapes scale and shreds soft roots, then flushes the debris down and out.

A cable machine opens a channel, and the pipe wall keeps its coating. That is why a line that clears and then clogs again in two months often needs jetting, not a third cable pass.

Cable or jetter: how we choose

We do not push a jetter on every job. The pipe and the problem decide.

Where jetting pays off in South St. Paul homes

Homes that predate 1970 make up the large majority of housing in South St. Paul, according to the city's 2040 planning documents. Their lines are often cast iron or clay, and decades of scale and roots narrow them.

Kitchen branch lines in any house can hold years of grease. Small restaurants and multifamily buildings add cooking oil and laundry lint to the mix. In each case jetting restores the pipe's working diameter more than a cable alone.
