What a camera inspection shows

We feed a small waterproof camera through the cleanout and watch the monitor as it travels toward the main. The camera records pipe material, joints, roots, cracks, offsets, low spots that hold water and any sign of collapse.

We note how far from the cleanout each issue sits, so you know whether a defect is under the house, the driveway or the lawn. You walk away with the video and a plain description of what you saw.

What we see and what it means

A camera finding is only useful if you know what to do with it.

FindingWhat it suggestsTypical next step

Inspections find problems at the time of the visit and do not predict the future.

When a sewer inspection is worth it

Not every home needs one, but these situations usually do.

  • You are buying an older house, especially one built before 1970
  • A drain has clogged more than twice in a year
  • You see mature trees close to the sewer line
  • Your yard has a damp or sunken stripe along the line
  • You are planning a basement bathroom and want to know the line can handle it

Buying or selling in South St. Paul

The city's 2040 planning documents put about 74 percent of local homes as built before 1970, and over 60 percent before 1960. A line from that period may be clay or cast iron, and a camera tells a buyer which one it is.

The City of South St. Paul runs a Time of Sale program, and the city office at 651-554-3220 can tell sellers what it requires. Ask your agent and the city early so an inspection result does not surprise anyone at closing.

What to do with the video

Keep the recording. If a defect shows up, send a copy to your insurer, your real estate agent or a second plumber for a second opinion. If the line is clean, the video is a baseline you can compare against in future years.

When a seller shares a video, check the date and make sure the camera reached the main. A short video that ends at the foundation shows little. Ask for the whole run.
